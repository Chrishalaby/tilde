The brief

Tilde is a first-person exploration game that runs in a browser tab. You walk, and you look; that is the whole verb list. No goals, no enemies, no score, no interface, and the land continues in every direction for as long as you care to go. Every metre is computed rather than stored, so the same seed and the same coordinates give you the same hill and the same monolith in ten years as today.

It is drawn with one font. The only asset in the repository is a single OFL monospace file; every texture, every note and the reverb tail are generated at boot. The picture is deliberately unsharp: low internal resolution, character cells instead of pixels, fog the colour of the paper it dissolves into, a palette held under fifteen percent saturation. Music arrives rarely and leaves long silences. The only hints are capital letters standing in the landscape, and nothing explains them.

Quiet

No goals, no HUD, no threat. Nothing asks you for anything.

Unsharp

Low resolution, glyph cells, heavy fog. Softness is the beauty, not a compromise.

Endless

A deterministic infinite world, streamed in chunks at the speed you walk.

Unchanging

Same seed plus same coordinates equals the same place, permanently.

One font

One monospace file is the whole asset list. The rest is generated.

Simple on top, complex underneath, means something specific here. On top: two movement keys, a mouse, a horizon. Underneath: a worker pool generating terrain off the main thread, a chunk manager with a seven-state lifecycle and LRU eviction, hand-written simplex noise with domain warp, a two-pass renderer whose second pass picks one character per cell from a measured font atlas, a generative audio scheduler on the two-clocks pattern, and persistence split across two storage APIs by how fast each piece of state grows. None of it is visible, and all of it exists so that nothing has to be.

Reference points

Five things this borrows from, and the part of each we are not taking.

  • Proteus (2013). Take the goal-less first person, and music that responds to where you are standing rather than to anything you achieved. Leave the saturated palette and the island's edges.
  • Return of the Obra Dinn. Take one lesson: a harsh visual constraint reads as elegance the moment the palette is disciplined enough to carry it. Leave the dithering, the monochrome and the withheld-information structure.
  • Acerola's ASCII shader (2024), and the older luminance-to-glyph shaders. Take the technique exactly: a glyph chosen by brightness, overridden by an edge glyph where a Sobel filter finds a direction, applied after the frame exists. Leave the neon demo look and the urge to make the effect the subject.
  • Dwarf Fortress. Take the proof that a field of characters can be read as landscape rather than as text. Leave the simulation, the systems, the menus and the losing.
  • Comanche-style heightfield raycasting. Take it for this document's own hero image, where a few hundred lines of Canvas 2D beat any engine. The game does not use it: a rasterised mesh gives us vertex colours, material ids and instanced props.

Decisions

These fifteen records are the case-study half of the document. Each names the forks that were genuinely open, the branch taken, the reasoning as it stood, and the price being paid. Later sections cite them by number, which is why they are labelled.

D1Two dimensions or three

Options
  • 2D top-down grid: simplest, instantly readable.
  • 2.5D isometric: depth without a real camera.
  • Full 3D, first person: a horizon.
Choice
Full 3D, first person.
Why
Walking toward something is the entire feeling being chased, and it needs a horizon and fog to exist at all. A top-down view can show a beautiful map, but never a far ridge that is seven minutes away.
Cost
Terrain meshing, a camera and height sampling under the eye: about one and a half times the code of the 2D plan.

D2A world built from glyphs, or a world drawn in glyphs

Options
  • Tiles as textured glyph quads: a literal character world.
  • Glyph picked per vertex in the terrain shader: cheap, tied to mesh density.
  • Render ordinary geometry, convert the frame afterwards.
Choice
Render untextured flat-shaded geometry with vertex colours into a low-resolution target, then pick one glyph per cell in a screen-space pass.
Why
The pass hides coarse geometry, so the 3D never has to be good, and that is the whole budget argument for one person. It keeps the font as the only texture in the build, and a world of glyph quads stops being a landscape the moment you turn the camera.
Cost
Two passes and a render target, and anything that must stay sharp, the journal and the map, lives outside the pass as DOM in the same font.

D3Framework, engine, or neither

Options
  • One HTML file, no build: nothing to install.
  • A UI framework: familiar structure.
  • An engine, Babylon or Godot web: batteries included.
  • Vite with TypeScript modules: a build step, nothing above it.
Choice
Vite plus TypeScript ES modules, no framework, no engine.
Why
A loop that runs sixty times a second does not want a reconciler sitting between it and the canvas. Engines hide exactly the parts that are the pleasure of this project: chunk streaming, the worker protocol, the glyph pass.
Cost
Everything a scene graph would have handed over is written by hand, and there is now a build step between the source and the page.

D4How close to the metal

Options
  • Raw WebGL2: total control, no dependency.
  • Three.js as a library: render targets, buffers, instancing, fog.
  • PixiJS: excellent, and two-dimensional.
Choice
Three.js, used as a library rather than as a framework, and the only runtime dependency.
Why
The interesting problems here are the generator and the glyph pass, not attribute binding and uniform plumbing. Three gives us WebGLRenderTarget, BufferGeometry, InstancedMesh and exponential fog without imposing a project structure.
Cost
A few hundred kilobytes of the bundle budget, and someone else's release cadence to track.

D5Frontend only, or a backend

Options
  • Frontend only: nothing to run, nothing to pay for.
  • A small API: cross-device saves, a shared board.
  • Full multiplayer: other walkers on the same seed.
Choice
Frontend only.
Why
The world is a pure function of its seed, so sharing one is sharing a link rather than replicating a database. The only features that would genuinely need a server, shared presence and a shared board, are out of scope for v1.
Cost
No cross-device saves, and no way to see that anyone else ever found the same letter.

D6Where it is hosted

Options
  • GitHub Pages: free, already where the code lives.
  • Cloudflare Pages or Netlify: free, custom headers.
  • A small VPS: full control, including headers.
Choice
Any static host, with GitHub Pages as the baseline that must keep working.
Why
The build output is static files and a font. The one thing that could rule a host out is needing COOP and COEP headers for cross-origin isolation, and D8 avoids that deliberately rather than by accident.
Cost
SharedArrayBuffer is off the table permanently, not just for now.

D7Store explored terrain, or regenerate it

Options
  • Write chunks to IndexedDB: instant revisits.
  • Memory cache only, regenerate on return: nothing to migrate.
  • A hybrid, storing only chunks the player lingered in.
Choice
Regenerate. A chunk is a pure function of (seed, cx, cz, GEN_VERSION), and no byte of terrain is ever written to storage.
Why
A stored world drifts out of step with the generator, and its quota fills the longer someone enjoys the game. Generation is under eight milliseconds in a worker, competitive with a database read once you add a schema and a migration path. It also makes the unchanging pillar a property of the code, not of a disk that might be cleared.
Cost
The generator becomes load-bearing. GEN_VERSION is pinned, saved with the seed, and old generators stay behind a switch so nobody's world shifts under them.

D8Where generation runs, and how the buffers move

Options
  • Main thread: no protocol, a hitch on every chunk.
  • Workers with structured clone: safe, copies everything.
  • Workers with SharedArrayBuffer: zero copy, shared memory.
  • Workers with transferable ArrayBuffers: zero copy, ownership moves.
Choice
A pool of hardwareConcurrency minus one, clamped to between two and four, passing transferable ArrayBuffers.
Why
A chunk is tens of kilobytes of typed arrays with a single owner at a time, which is precisely the case transfer exists for. SharedArrayBuffer buys concurrent reads we do not need and costs the isolation headers, which costs D6.
Cost
Once transferred, the worker's reference is dead, so every read happens before the post, and replies for the wrong chunk or a stale version have to be dropped.

D9Noise and hashing

Options
  • A noise library and a PRNG library: already tested.
  • Value noise by hand: trivial, visibly gridded.
  • Simplex with fBm and warp by hand: a few hundred lines we own.
Choice
Hand-written 2D simplex, fBm and domain warp in a dependency-free module, with splitmix-style 32-bit mixing of the coordinates seeding an sfc32 generator per chunk.
Why
The same module is imported by the main thread and by every worker, so it has to bundle into a worker without configuration archaeology. Determinism is the product here, and delegating it to a dependency's internal state and version history is an odd place to put that trust.
Cost
A few hundred lines to keep tested, and the gradient tables have to stay byte-identical on both sides of the worker boundary.

D10Persistence

Options
  • localStorage only: synchronous, five megabytes, a string API.
  • IndexedDB only: asynchronous, large, ceremonious.
  • Both, split by role.
Choice
Both. localStorage for tiny synchronous state, IndexedDB for anything that grows without a ceiling.
Why
Settings, seed and pose must be readable before the first frame and writable inside a pagehide handler, which is what synchronous storage is good at. Discoveries and the visited-chunk set grow for as long as someone keeps walking, which is what IndexedDB is good at.
Cost
Two storage modules, and two failure modes to handle quietly: quota exhaustion and private browsing.

D11Audio from files or from code

Options
  • Recorded ambient loops: fastest route to sounding good.
  • Sampled instruments triggered by code: still megabytes of assets.
  • Fully generative Web Audio: no assets, total control, no fallback.
Choice
Fully generative Web Audio, down to a reverb impulse response made from four seconds of exponentially decaying noise.
Why
One font as the only asset is a rule, not a slogan, and a folder of audio files would quietly end it. Pads with multi-second attacks through a lowpass at 1.2 kHz are a handful of nodes, so the synthesis is not the hard part; restraint is.
Cost
It will never sound recorded, and anything richer than a pad or a bell is real work rather than a file.

D12Which font, and how it becomes an atlas

Options
  • A system monospace stack: zero bytes, different on every machine.
  • A bundled OFL TTF, rasterised at boot: identical everywhere.
  • A hand-drawn bitmap font: exact control, a week of pixel art.
Choice
Bundle IBM Plex Mono under the OFL, rasterise it into a glyph atlas with Canvas 2D at boot, measure each glyph's ink coverage, and sort the ramps by the measurement.
Why
A ramp only works if each glyph is genuinely darker than the one before it, and that is a property of the rasterisation at the actual cell size, not of anyone's intuition about how heavy a semicolon looks. Measuring at boot means swapping the font later does not mean rewriting five ramps by hand.
Cost
A boot step before the first frame, and about a tenth of the bundle budget spent on one file.

D13Camera

Options
  • First person: presence, a horizon, and pointer lock.
  • Third person behind a wandering figure: charming, readable.
  • Free fly: wonderful for debugging, weightless for playing.
Choice
First person, eye at 1.6 m, walking at 2.5 m/s with everything eased.
Why
Presence is the point, and a body seen from behind puts a character between the player and the place. The low eye height and the deliberate slowness are what make a ridge a kilometre away feel like seven minutes rather than a texture on the horizon.
Cost
Pointer lock, which a real share of people dislike, mitigated by arrow-key turning and one line of prompting.

D14TypeScript or JavaScript

Options
  • JavaScript with JSDoc: no build friction, hints where they help.
  • TypeScript in strict mode: a compiler that reads the worker protocol.
Choice
TypeScript, strict.
Why
The worker message contract and ChunkData are the two places where a silent shape mismatch produces a world that is subtly wrong rather than obviously broken, and subtly wrong is expensive to chase. Types make the transfer list and the typed-array widths explicit at the call site.
Cost
Some wrestling with Three.js and worker typings, and very little else.

D15What gets tested

Options
  • Nothing: it is a toy, and the screen is the test.
  • Vitest on the pure modules only.
  • Vitest plus Playwright: real frames, real maintenance.
Choice
Vitest on the pure modules only: determinism, hash distribution, and ramp ordering.
Why
The determinism test earns its keep, generating the same chunk twice and across the worker boundary and comparing the bytes, because that is the pillar a refactor breaks without anyone noticing. Ramp ordering catches a font change that has inverted the tonal scale.
Cost
Nothing in the renderer, the audio or the controller is covered, which is accepted: those are judged by looking and listening.

The look

Two passes, one font

Cells and softness

The screen is a grid of character cells, 10 by 18 CSS pixels by default and tunable from 8 by 14 up to 14 by 26. One glyph per cell, one ink, one paper, and no detail finer than a cell exists anywhere.

The scene renders at two scene pixels per cell on each axis. At 1920 by 1080 that is 192 by 60 cells and a 384 by 120 target, about one forty-fifth of the pixels in the window. Two is the right number: one texel per cell leaves the edge filter nothing to work with, and four starts to resolve the coarse geometry the pass exists to hide.

Sizes are CSS pixels. The device pixel ratio is ignored on purpose: a denser display buys nothing when the image has no detail finer than a cell.

The two passes

The scene pass draws the world into one render target. Flat shading, no textures, vertex colours carrying the biome ink, one directional light for the sun, and exponential fog whose colour is exactly the paper of the moment, so distance dissolves into the page rather than into a horizon. The sky is plain paper, the same clear colour. Output is RGBA: shaded colour in RGB, material id as a byte in alpha. Nearest filtering, no mipmaps, because any filter that smooths the target smears material ids across boundaries and invents inks that do not exist.

The glyph pass is a full-screen triangle with the scene target and the atlas bound. It decides one glyph per cell and writes final pixels. There is no third pass: grain and vignette happen at the end of the same shader.

Choosing a glyph

  1. Average the 2 by 2 block of scene texels under the cell for luminance L.
  2. Take the material id M from the alpha of the texel nearest the cell centre, never a blend of four.
  3. Sobel the low-resolution luminance for edge strength and direction.
  4. If the edge is strong, quantise its direction to four and pick from the edge set.
  5. Otherwise, if M is water, pick from the water cycle on time and a per-cell hash, so the surface moves while the camera stands still.
  6. Otherwise index the ramp for M by L, nudged by a per-cell dither so boundaries break up instead of drawing a seam.
  7. Sample the atlas at the cell-local position for coverage, mix paper toward ink[M] by it, then grain and vignette. 
vec2 cell   = floor(gl_FragCoord.xy / cellPx);
vec2 uv     = (cell + 0.5) * cellPx / resolution;
vec4 s      = texture2D(sceneTex, uv);
float L     = dot(s.rgb, vec3(0.299, 0.587, 0.114));
int   M     = int(s.a * 255.0 + 0.5);
vec2  g     = sobel(sceneTex, uv);
float e     = length(g);
int idx;
if (e > EDGE_THRESHOLD) {
    idx = edgeGlyph(atan(g.y, g.x));
} else if (M == MAT_WATER) {
    idx = waterGlyph(time, hash2(cell));
} else {
    idx = rampGlyph(M, L + dither(cell) * 0.04);
}
vec2  inCell = fract(gl_FragCoord.xy / cellPx);
float cov    = texture2D(atlas, atlasUV(idx, inCell)).r;
vec3  col    = mix(paper, ink[M], cov);
col += (hash2(gl_FragCoord.xy + timeSeed) - 0.5) * 0.03;
col *= vignette(uv);
gl_FragColor = vec4(col, 1.0);

The letters

The world is written in punctuation. Every glyph the terrain can make is a mark, with one exception: a monolith carries a single capital fixed by its region hash, legible from far off because nothing else on screen is letter-shaped.

Press J and the journal shows the alphabet, the letters you have stood next to filled in and the rest faint. No counter, no percentage, no congratulation, and nothing ever explains what it is for. A player who notices has a reason to walk for months. That is the whole long game, for one overlay and one store.

What is deliberately absent

  • No bloom. It exists to make bright pixels feel bright, and the brightest thing here is a filled character.
  • No chromatic aberration. A lens artefact, and there is no lens.
  • No HUD. The seed shows in tiny type at the bottom left for a few seconds after load, then goes.
  • No textures. One font file is the only asset in the repository, and every surface is flat vertex colour until the pass gives it a texture made of characters.
  • No sharp edges. Nothing is antialiased, and no pass tries to recover detail the target threw away.

The world

Scale

One unit is one metre, in every file. The numbers below are the whole spatial budget, chosen together: chunks small enough to build in three milliseconds, a render radius wide enough that the fog ends before the chunks do, and a prefetch ring one chunk beyond that.

QuantityValueNote
Chunk64 × 64 mAddressed by integer (cx, cz)
Vertex spacing2 mCoarse on purpose. The glyph pass hides it.
Vertices per chunk33 × 33 = 1,0892,048 triangles, edges shared with neighbours
Render radius6 chunks, 384 mAbout 169 chunks visible at most
Prefetch radius7 chunks, 448 mOne ring of work always ahead of the eye
Fog start120 mExponential, colour equals paper
Fog end360 mInside the render radius, so chunks never pop in
Region512 × 512 m8 × 8 chunks, the unit landmarks live in

At 2.5 m/s a ridge a kilometre off is about seven minutes away, and there is no sprint. That is the point, and every number above exists to hold those seven minutes at 60 frames per second.

Height

Four layers of noise, summed, with the ridged layer gated so mountains grow only on land. Without that gate, ridges run out into the sea and the coastline turns to gravel.

LayerWavelengthAmplitudeRole
Continent~2 km±110 mDecides land and sea, and nothing else
Ridges~400 mup to +90 mRidged noise, only where continent > 0
Hills~100 m±18 mThe shape you actually walk over
Detail~12 m±1.5 mTexture for the edge filter to find
Domain warp~700 m~60 m offsetApplied to x and z before any layer is sampled

The warp is the cheapest thing on this page. Plain summed noise makes blobs that read as noise. Bending the input coordinates first gives valleys that bend and coastlines that are not circles. 

export function heightAt(x: number, z: number): number {
  const wx = x + warp(x, z, 700) * 60;
  const wz = z + warp(z + 811.3, x - 47.9, 700) * 60;
  const continent = fbm(wx / 2000, wz / 2000, 4) * 110;
  let h = continent;
  if (continent > 0) {
    const r = ridged(wx / 400, wz / 400, 3);
    h += r * 90 * smoothstep(0, 25, continent);
  }
  h += fbm(wx / 100, wz / 100, 3) * 18;
  h += fbm(wx / 12, wz / 12, 2) * 1.5;
  return h;
}

Sea level is zero. The water plane sits at y = 0 with a ripple in the vertex shader, which never touches the height field or where you can walk.

Props

Trees are what make a forest read. Without them, forest is a slightly darker grassland and nobody notices entering one. Each forest chunk is walked on a 3 metre grid, a hash decides with probability 0.3 whether a tree stands at a cell, and its height comes from the chunk's own height array. They are instanced cones on thin trunk lines, one InstancedMesh per chunk. The pass sees only dark cones with strong edges, which is what produces the ^ Y T ! silhouettes.

The ground under a tree stays grass. Nothing is carved into the height field and no prop is load-bearing for collision. Rocks follow the same rule, far sparser, on stone.

Landmarks

The world is divided into 512 metre regions, and a hash of the region coordinates gives one in nine a landmark. Close enough that a long walk usually finds something, far enough that finding it counts.

Five kinds. The letter monolith is the most common, its letter the region hash modulo 26, then a stone ring, a lone tall tree, a still pool and a shelter. Each is a few instanced boxes or cones, because the pass reduces it to a dozen cells anyway.

Placement is the part that matters. A landmark goes at the highest local point within 100 metres of the region centre, not at the centre, so on broken ground it sits on a crest and breaks the fog line. You see it from far off and choose to walk to it. Each discovery is written to IndexedDB under the region key, which is how the journal fills.

Deferred

Rivers, caves, weather, animals and buildings are out. Each wants a system the rest of the world does not have: flow accumulation, a second mesh topology, a simulation clock, behaviour. What ships is only terrain.

Movement has one job, to make distance felt without making it tedious. Everything is eased and there is no speed above a walk.

Ground height is a bilinear sample of the current chunk's height array, the same Float32Array the worker produced. No physics engine and no collider: the player is a point that reads a number and sits above it.

Water and slopes push back rather than stop you. You can wade to 1.2 metres, and past that the part of your movement heading deeper falls smoothly to zero, so the sea refuses you without a wall and without a message. Slopes over about 45 degrees scale your speed down instead of blocking it, so a mountain is climbable if you are patient.

Drift mode, on F, hands the walker to the game. It picks a heading toward the open horizon, steers away from water and from slopes it would rather not climb, and every so often stops to look before choosing again. It never arrives, and any input takes control back. This is for the second monitor, and also the fastest way to find out whether the world is boring.

Sound

Rules of restraint

Silence is the feature. After every six to ten notes the scheduler takes a mandatory break of 20 to 60 seconds, and during it the only sound is wind and, if you are near it, water. A note is considered about every 3.2 seconds and plays with probability 0.35, so even the talkative stretches are mostly gaps. Music that always plays becomes wallpaper, then an irritant.

Scales by place

The scale follows the biome under the player and changes on the next note, not at the boundary, so a crossing is never audible as a switch.

PlaceScaleCharacter
GrasslandC major pentatonicOpen. No leading tone, so nothing wants to resolve.
ForestC major pentatonic, low drone on the rootWeight underneath, the notes unchanged
Near waterPentatonic plus F♯A lydian colour, the one bright note in the game
StoneA minor pentatonicThe same pitches rearranged. Darker for free.
SnowA minor pentatonic, one octave upThin, and sparser still

Instruments

Two. The pad is a sine and a triangle detuned slightly against each other, attack 2 to 4 seconds, release 6 to 10, through a lowpass at about 1.2 kHz, so no note has an onset you could tap along to. The bell is a small two-operator FM voice with a long tail, and it plays in one situation: a landmark has just been discovered. It is the only sound that means anything, so it stays rare.

Scheduling

The two-clocks pattern, because a JavaScript timer is not accurate enough to place a note and the audio clock cannot run your logic. A setInterval at 25 milliseconds wakes, looks 100 milliseconds ahead on currentTime, and schedules anything inside that window at a precise audio time. The timer decides when to think, never when a sound happens. 

const LOOKAHEAD = 0.1;
const TICK_MS = 25;

function tick(ctx: AudioContext, s: SchedulerState) {
  while (s.nextNoteAt < ctx.currentTime + LOOKAHEAD) {
    if (s.nextNoteAt < s.silentUntil) {
      s.nextNoteAt += NOTE_GRID;
      continue;
    }
    if (s.rng() < 0.35) {
      playPad(ctx, pickNote(scaleFor(s.place)), s.nextNoteAt);
      if (--s.notesLeft <= 0) beginSilence(s, s.nextNoteAt);
    }
    s.nextNoteAt += NOTE_GRID;
  }
}

Ambience

Wind is pink noise through a lowpass, always running, gain rising with altitude and walking speed. It is what makes a ridge feel exposed. Water is a second noise source gated on being within 30 metres, gain by proximity. Both feed a ConvolverNode whose impulse response is generated at boot, four seconds of noise with an exponential decay.

Browser rules

No AudioContext exists until the first user gesture, because browsers will not allow it and because starting silent is correct anyway. Master gain sits at −12 dBFS, low enough to leave on, and the volume keys move that one node. No mixer and no mute button, because - held down is one.

Architecture

The shape of the program is a loop and a pool. The loop runs on the main thread and is allowed two jobs: react to the person, and draw. The pool is two to four workers with one job: turn three numbers into a chunk. Everything in this section follows from keeping that line clean.

Threads

The split sits at the purity boundary, not at the busy boundary. chunk-gen.ts takes (seed, cx, cz, genVersion) and returns plain arrays. It reads no shared state, touches no DOM, allocates its own output, and has no opinion about when it runs. That is the entire reason it can live somewhere else.

Everything left on the main thread fails one of those tests. The scene graph, the AudioContext, the canvas, pointer lock, the stores and the player's pose are single-owner state, and pushing any of them across a message boundary would buy a protocol and a fresh class of bugs for nothing. The main thread only draws and reacts; the workers only compute.

The main thread is allowed to be busy, not blocked, and generation is the only part here that would block it: roughly 8 ms per chunk, and one border crossing can want a dozen at once. The pool is hardwareConcurrency - 1 clamped to 2 and 4 (D8). One core stays for the loop, and past four the workers queue against memory bandwidth. They start at boot, because the first crossing is the one you notice.

The worker contract

Two message types, one of them a reply, and nothing else crosses the line (D14 exists mostly for this file):

export type GenRequest = {
  type: 'gen';
  seed: number;
  cx: number;
  cz: number;
  genVersion: number;
};

export type Landmark = {
  kind: 'monolith' | 'ring' | 'tree' | 'pool' | 'shelter';
  letter?: string;
  x: number;
  z: number;
};

export type ChunkData = {
  cx: number;
  cz: number;
  genVersion: number;
  heights: Float32Array;
  materials: Uint8Array;
  props: Float32Array;
  landmark: Landmark | null;
};

export type GenReply = { type: 'chunk' } & ChunkData;

export function transfers(c: ChunkData): Transferable[] {
  return [c.heights.buffer, c.materials.buffer, c.props.buffer];
}

Every array in a reply is allocated in the worker and posted in the transfer list, so the buffers detach there and arrive here without a copy. A chunk is about 4.3 KB of heights, 1.1 KB of materials and a few KB of props. Copying that would survive; transferring makes the cost independent of how fast you walk, and it enforces a rule worth having: a worker never keeps a reference to what it sent. Fresh arrays every call, no pooling.

Replies are validated on arrival, not on request. If (cx, cz) has left the prefetch radius, or genVersion no longer matches config, the reply is dropped and its buffers become garbage. There is no cancel message: a worker mid-job finishes it, because coordinating a cancellation costs more than the 8 ms it saves.

Chunk lifecycle

A chunk holds exactly one state, and the transitions are the manager. Each frame the desired set is rebuilt: every coordinate inside the prefetch radius of 7, sorted by distance, and anything in it without a record becomes wanted. Wanted chunks enter the queue in distance order, and queued becomes generating the moment a worker is free, so priority lives in the queue rather than in a worker's inbox. A reply makes the chunk ready: arrays on the main thread, nothing uploaded yet.

Ready becomes built when terrain-mesh and props run, at most two chunks per frame and each under 3 ms. That budget is what keeps a border crossing from becoming a hitch: a backlog costs latency, which fog hides, while a long frame costs smoothness, which nothing hides. Built becomes visible inside the render radius of 6.

Visible and hidden flip freely. Hidden keeps its geometry and only detaches the mesh, because re-adding is free and rebuilding is not. Eviction is the last resort: once retained chunks pass twice the visible count, the least recently visible hidden chunk is disposed, geometry and heights together. Nothing is written on the way out (D7); coming back means generating it again, byte for byte the same.

Frame order

One requestAnimationFrame callback, always in this order, because later steps read what earlier ones wrote:

  1. Drain input: key state, accumulated mouse delta, pointer-lock changes, one-shot keys such as M, J and F.
  2. Step the player: accelerate toward the wanted velocity, sample terrain height bilinearly, set the eye 1.6 m above it, apply the slope and wading rules.
  3. Ease the camera: position lags by about 0.15 s, yaw and pitch ease separately, so the world settles after you stop rather than with you.
  4. Rebuild the desired set from the new position and post requests to idle workers, nearest first.
  5. Drain replies: validate, keep or drop, build at most two chunks, add or remove meshes, evict past the LRU limit.
  6. Advance time of day: fog colour, sun direction, paper and ink, plus the water and glyph animation uniforms.
  7. Render: the scene into the target at roughly 290 × 90 px, then the glyph pass onto the canvas.
  8. Bookkeeping: hand the scheduler the current biome, altitude and speed (it keeps its own clock), redraw an open overlay, mark the save dirty for the 5 s debounce.

Memory and sharing

The world is never saved, because it cannot be lost. What is saved is small, and splits by whether it has a bound (D10): settings and pose are a handful of numbers, while discoveries and visited chunks grow for as long as you walk.

Key or storeStoreContentsWhen written
tilde.settingslocalStoragecell size, volume, reduce grain, head bobon change, 5 s debounce
tilde.worldlocalStorageseed (base36), genVersiononce, when a world is first opened
tilde.poselocalStoragex, z, yaw, pitch, timeOfDay5 s debounce and on pagehide

A world lives in its URL. The seed is read from ?seed=<base36>; if it is absent, boot rolls a random 32-bit seed and writes it back with history.replaceState, without a reload, so the address bar holds a shareable link before the first frame lands. Nothing else goes in the URL: no pose, no time of day, no discoveries. A link is a place, not a state of mind.

Two people with the same seed stand in the same world and keep their own journals. That is exactly as much multiplayer as this game wants (D5).

GEN_VERSION is an integer in config.ts, written into tilde.world when a world is first opened and never rewritten. It is the promise behind the unchanging pillar, and any edit that moves a vertex breaks it: a new octave, a different warp offset, a corrected sign, a nudged biome threshold. So the rule is: bump the integer, keep the old generator in the source, and switch on the version stored with the save, so a world made on version 3 keeps calling version 3 code forever. Old generators are pure functions with no dependencies, so keeping one costs a file and a case in a switch. The alternative, a migration or a friendly dialog, is a way of telling somebody their world is gone.

Build order

  1. M0 Scaffold, flat plane, glyph pass. Vite and TypeScript strict, the atlas rasterised at boot with ramps sorted by coverage, a render target, and the glyph pass over one plane and one cone. Done when both read as glyphs with fog and the day palette at 60 fps.
  2. M1 Chunked terrain in workers, walking. Hash and noise, the pure generator, the pool, the manager with its states and LRU, terrain meshing, and a controller that samples height. Done when you can walk five minutes in any direction at 60 fps and turning around gives back exactly the terrain you left.
  3. M2 Biomes, props, day cycle, water. Material ids and per-biome inks, instanced trees and rocks, the four palettes with their 90 s crossfades, the water plane and its ripple. Done when a screenshot could be mistaken for the hero at the top of this page.
  4. M3 Audio. The two-clock scheduler, pads on the per-biome scales, wind and water beds, the generated reverb, and the silence rule that makes all of it bearable. Done when you can leave it running for an hour in a window you are not looking at.
  5. M4 Persistence, seed URL, journal, map, landmarks. Region hashing and landmark placement, discovery detection, both stores, the two overlays. Done when a reload puts you back where you stood, and a friend opening your link sees your world.
  6. M5 Polish: easing, drift mode, settings, grain, hosting. Camera lag and eased turning, drift mode on F, the settings the keys reach, grain and vignette tuning, a static deploy. Done when it is on a URL you would send to somebody without explaining it.

The core is M0 to M2, and for anyone who has written a game loop before that is a weekend of evenings: the glyph pass is one shader, the manager is a state machine over a map, the noise stack is two hundred lines with tests. M3 to M5 are a few more evenings, mostly typing. The honest note is that M2 and M3 are not where the code is hard, they are where taste decides. Ramp order, cell size, where the fog starts, how often a note lands: those get tuned by standing in the world and changing one number at a time, which is most of the calendar and almost none of the work.

Open calls

  • The name. Tilde is a placeholder that has started to sound right, which is how placeholders win. Does the water glyph keep the title, or does the title stop being an in-joke about punctuation before anyone else reads it?
  • Night: invert or dim? A true inversion, pale ink on dark paper, is the best frame in the palette and the only one that stops looking like a page; dimming keeps the paper metaphor for the whole cycle. Which matters more at three in the morning?
  • Do the letters spell anything? A hidden word pays off the long game once and turns every later monolith into an errand; an alphabet that stays an alphabet keeps the quiet and risks amounting to nothing. Is the reward the word, or the filled-in page?
  • Default cell size. 10 × 18 is the default and [ and ] move it. Bigger cells are softer, calmer and cheaper; smaller cells show the terrain that was actually built. Which one should somebody get in their first thirty seconds, before they know the keys exist?
  • A tiny compass, or nothing? A compass makes "go back the way you came" possible and gives the map overlay an anchor; it is also the first piece of HUD on a screen whose whole argument is that it has none. Is direction worth the exception?

All of the above is deliberately over-specified, so that the build can start on a Friday evening without re-deciding anything first. Every number in it is a starting value and not a law: change them in config.ts, from inside the world, while walking.
